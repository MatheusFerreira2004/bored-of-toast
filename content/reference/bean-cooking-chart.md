## Abertura (124 palavras)

Recipes rarely agree on beans. One calls for a can, another for a cup of dried, a third for cooked weight in grams. They are describing the same thing at three different stages, and converting between them is where most home cooks give up and buy the can.

The conversion is simple once you see it written down. A standard can of beans, drained, gives you about one and a half cups. Getting to that same amount from dried takes roughly two thirds of a cup and some patience.

Below are the cooking times for the legumes worth keeping in a pantry, the conversion table between dried, canned and cooked, and the two rules about salt and acid that change the result more than any timing chart will.

---

## The conversion rule

Bloco de destaque.

**One can (15 oz / 425 g), drained:** about 1½ cups cooked beans
**To match it from dried:** about ⅔ cup (130 g) dried beans
**One cup dried yields:** 2½ to 3 cups cooked

Nota de exemplo:
A recipe calling for two cans of chickpeas needs about 1⅓ cups dried. Start the soak the night before.

## Nota editorial sobre a divergência

Esta seção é o diferencial da página. Manter.

Sources disagree on this conversion, and the disagreement is worth explaining. Serious Eats puts the dried equivalent of one can at three quarters of a cup. Earth to Veg and Rustic Roots Living both say half a cup. Reluctant Gourmet says a full cup.

They are not contradicting each other so much as measuring different things. Half a cup of dried beans yields roughly a cup and a quarter cooked, which is slightly short of a drained can. A full cup of dried yields close to three cups, which is nearly two cans.

Two thirds of a cup sits where the arithmetic actually lands, and it is the number used in the table above. If a recipe is forgiving, any of these will work. If you are cooking for an exact yield, weigh rather than measure by volume.

## Os dois erros que mais custam

Bloco de exceções, dois itens.

### 1. Adding acid too early

Lemon, vinegar and tomatoes firm up bean skins. Added at the start, they can keep beans tough well past the point where they should have softened — sometimes indefinitely. Cook the beans until tender first, then add anything acidic.

This is why a bean and tomato stew that simmers from cold so often has chalky beans at the end.

### 2. Salting at the wrong moment

The old advice to never salt the cooking water has been overturned. Salt in the water from the start seasons the bean itself rather than just the liquid around it, and softens the skin rather than toughening it.

The texture difference between beans salted at the start and beans salted at the end is the single biggest improvement available to anyone cooking dried beans.

## Bloco de segurança

Obrigatório. Feijão-vermelho cru contém fitohemaglutinina em níveis que causam intoxicação.

**Kidney beans need a hard boil.** Raw and undercooked kidney beans contain a natural toxin that causes severe nausea and vomiting within hours. Slow cooking alone does not reach a temperature high enough to destroy it — beans cooked at low temperature can be more dangerous than raw ones.

- Soak kidney beans for at least five hours
- Discard the soaking water
- Boil hard for at least 10 minutes in fresh water before reducing to a simmer
- Do not cook raw kidney beans in a slow cooker without boiling them first

This applies to red kidney beans in particular, and to a lesser degree to white kidney beans and cannellini. Lentils, chickpeas and black beans do not carry the same risk.

Discard soaking water for all beans. It carries the compounds most often blamed for digestive discomfort.
