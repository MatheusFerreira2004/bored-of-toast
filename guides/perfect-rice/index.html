How to Cook Perfect Rice, Every Time

Simple stovetop methods, reliable ratios, and quick fixes for when rice goes mushy or crunchy.

Photo: Tuğba on Pexels

Rice should be the easiest thing on the table, yet it is one of the most common kitchen frustrations. Gummy clumps, crunchy centers, a scorched layer stuck to the pot. The good news is that perfect rice is not luck. It comes down to three things: the right ratio of water to rice, steady low heat, and patience while it rests.

In this guide we walk through our go-to stovetop method for white rice, explain why brown rice needs different treatment, break down the difference between basmati and jasmine, and share a ratio table you can bookmark. We also cover how to rescue rice that went wrong, because it happens to all of us.

Key takeaways

  • Rinse white rice until the water runs mostly clear.
  • Use a tight lid and the lowest heat, and do not peek.
  • Always rest rice off the heat for 10 minutes before fluffing.
  • Brown rice needs more water and roughly twice the time.

Our Foolproof Stovetop White Rice

This method works for long-grain white rice like jasmine or basmati. You need a heavy-bottomed pot with a tight-fitting lid. A thin pot creates hot spots that scorch the bottom before the top is cooked.

Plan on about 1 cup of dry rice for 2 to 3 people. It roughly triples in volume as it cooks.

  • Rinse: Put 1 cup rice in a bowl, cover with cold water, swish, and drain. Repeat 2 to 3 times until the water is only slightly cloudy.
  • Combine: Add the rice, 1 1/2 cups water, and 1/2 teaspoon salt to the pot.
  • Boil: Bring to a boil over medium-high heat, uncovered, and give it one gentle stir.
  • Simmer: Cover, drop the heat to the lowest setting, and cook for 15 minutes without lifting the lid.
  • Rest: Turn off the heat and leave it covered for 10 minutes. The steam finishes the job.
  • Fluff: Use a fork or rice paddle to lift and separate the grains before serving.

Why Rinsing and Resting Matter

Rinsing washes away loose surface starch. That starch is what turns cooking water into glue and makes rice clump. Skip it for risotto or rice pudding, where you want creaminess, but rinse for fluffy side-dish rice.

Resting is the step most people skip. Right after cooking, the grains at the bottom are wetter than the top. Ten minutes off the heat lets moisture redistribute, so every grain is evenly tender and the rice releases cleanly from the pot.

Brown Rice Needs a Different Plan

Brown rice still has its bran layer, which is great for fiber and nutty flavor but slows down how fast water gets in. It needs more water and a lot more time, usually 40 to 45 minutes of simmering plus a 10 minute rest.

Our favorite shortcut is the pasta method. Boil brown rice in a big pot of salted water for about 30 minutes, drain it well, return it to the pot, cover, and let it steam off the heat for 10 minutes. You do not need to measure the water, and the grains come out distinct and never gummy.

Basmati vs. Jasmine: What Is the Difference?

Both are fragrant long-grain rices, and they are often used interchangeably, but they behave a little differently in the pot and on the plate.

  • Basmati: Long, slender grains that get even longer as they cook. Dry, separate, and fluffy with a nutty aroma. Best for pilafs, biryani, and curries. Soaking for 20 to 30 minutes before cooking gives extra-long grains.
  • Jasmine: Slightly shorter and plumper, with a floral scent. Softer and a little sticky, so it is easy to eat with chopsticks. Great with stir-fries and Thai or Vietnamese dishes. It needs slightly less water than basmati.
  • Short-grain and sushi rice: Round grains with more starch, meant to be sticky. Rinse well and use a bit less water than long-grain.

How to Fix Rice That Went Wrong

Do not throw it out. Most rice mistakes can be rescued, or at least repurposed into something delicious.

  • Crunchy in the middle: Add 2 to 3 tablespoons of water, cover tightly, and cook on the lowest heat for 5 more minutes, then rest.
  • Water left in the pot: Drain what you can, then return to low heat uncovered for a few minutes to steam off the rest.
  • Mushy or gummy: Spread it on a sheet pan and bake at 300°F (150°C) for 5 to 10 minutes to dry it out. Or lean in and make fried rice, rice pudding, or soup.
  • Burned on the bottom: Do not scrape. Spoon the good rice off the top into a clean bowl. Place a slice of bread on top for a few minutes to absorb any smoky smell.
  • Bland: Next time, salt the water and try cooking it in stock, or add a bay leaf or a knob of butter.

Questions

Can I double the recipe?

Yes. Double the rice and water, but use a wider pot so the rice layer is not too deep. The simmer time stays about the same, maybe a couple of minutes longer.

How do I store and reheat leftover rice safely?

Cool it quickly and refrigerate within an hour of cooking. Use it within 3 to 4 days. Reheat with a splash of water, covered, until steaming hot all the way through.

Why does my rice always stick to the pot?

Usually the heat is too high or the pot is too thin. Use the lowest setting during the simmer and always let the rice rest before serving so it releases.
