Knife Skills 101: Chop Faster and Safer

How to choose, hold, and care for a knife, plus the handful of cuts that make everyday cooking quicker.

Photo: Michael Burrows on Pexels

Good knife skills are the single biggest time-saver in a home kitchen. When chopping an onion takes you two minutes instead of ten, weeknight cooking stops feeling like a chore. Evenly cut pieces also cook evenly, so your food simply turns out better.

You do not need a chef's roll full of expensive blades or restaurant-level speed. You need one sharp knife, a stable board, and a few habits that keep your fingers safe. In this guide we cover the knives worth owning, how to grip them, the core cuts, and how to keep your edge sharp. Slow down, practice a little every time you cook, and speed will come on its own.

Key takeaways

  • A sharp knife is safer than a dull one.
  • Pinch the blade and curl your guiding fingers into a claw.
  • Anchor your board with a damp towel so it never slides.
  • Hone often, sharpen a few times a year, and never use the dishwasher.

The Only Three Knives You Really Need

Knife block sets look impressive, but most of those knives never leave the block. Put your money into one good chef's knife and add two helpers.

  • Chef's knife (8 inch / 20 cm): Your everyday workhorse for chopping, slicing, dicing, and mincing. Pick one that feels balanced and comfortable in your hand.
  • Paring knife (3 to 4 inch): For small, detailed jobs like hulling strawberries, trimming, and peeling.
  • Serrated bread knife: Saws through crusty bread, tomatoes, and cakes without crushing them.
  • Nice to have: A sturdy cutting board, wood or plastic, at least 12 by 18 inches. Skip glass, stone, and marble, which destroy edges.

Set Up Your Station and Grip

Safety starts before you cut anything. Place a damp paper towel or thin kitchen towel under your cutting board so it cannot slip. Keep your board clear, with a bowl nearby for scraps.

For the knife hand, use the pinch grip. Pinch the flat of the blade just in front of the handle between your thumb and the side of your index finger, then wrap your remaining three fingers around the handle. It feels odd at first, but it gives far more control than holding the handle alone.

For the guiding hand, make a claw. Curl your fingertips under and rest your knuckles against the side of the blade. The knife can then glide against your knuckles, and your fingertips stay safely tucked away.

The Basic Motions

Most cutting uses a smooth rocking or forward-slicing motion, not a straight chop downward. Let the tip of the knife stay near the board and roll the blade forward and down through the food. Let the sharp edge do the work instead of forcing it.

Before cutting anything round, like an onion, potato, or carrot, slice a thin piece off one side to create a flat base. Stable food is safe food. A rolling vegetable is the number one cause of kitchen cuts.

Core Cuts and Sizes

Recipes use a handful of terms for cut size. Consistency matters more than precision, since same-size pieces cook at the same rate. Here is a quick reference.

How to Dice an Onion

Master this one and half your prep is covered. The root holds the layers together, so leave it on until the end.

  • Halve: Cut the onion in half from the root to the tip. Peel off the skin and trim the tip end, leaving the root intact.
  • Slice lengthwise: Place a half flat side down. Make vertical cuts toward the root, stopping just short of it.
  • Optional horizontal cuts: Make one or two cuts parallel to the board toward the root for a finer dice.
  • Cross-cut: Slice across your earlier cuts, moving your claw hand back as you go. Perfect little cubes will fall away.
  • Finish: Discard the root end, or save it with other scraps for stock.

Keep Your Knife Sharp

A dull knife slips off food instead of biting into it, and that is when accidents happen. It also crushes herbs and tomatoes rather than slicing them cleanly.

A honing steel does not sharpen. It straightens the tiny edge that bends as you work. Give your knife a few strokes on each side every few uses. Actual sharpening removes metal to create a new edge, and most home cooks need it only two to four times a year. Use a whetstone, a quality pull-through sharpener, or a local sharpening service.

Finally, wash knives by hand and dry them right away. Dishwashers dull and chip the edge and can crack wooden handles. Store knives on a magnetic strip, in a block, or with blade guards, never loose in a drawer.

Questions

What should I do if I cut myself?

For minor cuts, rinse with clean water, apply firm pressure with a clean cloth until the bleeding stops, and cover with a bandage. Seek medical care for deep cuts, cuts that will not stop bleeding, or any sign of infection.

Wood or plastic cutting board?

Both are fine and both are gentle on knives. Many cooks keep a plastic board for raw meat since it can be sanitized easily, and a wooden board for everything else.

How do I know when my knife needs sharpening?

Try slicing a ripe tomato or a sheet of paper. If the blade slides off the tomato skin or tears the paper instead of cutting cleanly, it is time.
